3. The 5 Rules We Write By

  1. Short answer first. Every answer opens with one sentence a leader could repeat in a meeting: "Yes, on your existing BigQuery, in about 3 months."
  2. Their words, not ours. They say "controlled copy", "launch gate", "safe data". We use exactly those phrases, so they can see each principle being met.
  3. Two levels wherever they asked for two. Q2 (3 vs 6 months), Q4 (leadership vs wider team) and Q9 (us vs in house). Always both.
  4. Every assumption written down. We don't know the leadership team size, the identity provider or the accuracy bar. So we state our assumption and say what would change if it's wrong.
  5. No jargon without a translation. The readers are Operations & IT Systems and the leadership team, not data engineers. "Semantic layer" is fine once, if the same sentence says "one agreed definition of each KPI".

Q1 · Feasibility and approach

Scoring: can you do it on our BigQuery, and can you explain it without jargon?

Yes. We would build Phase 1 directly on your existing BigQuery warehouse and dbt models, without replacing anything you already run.

In plain terms there are four layers:

  1. A controlled copy. A separate BigQuery dataset, ai_safe, holds copies of approved, modelled tables only (sites, companies, members without personal details, contracts by month, bookings, the finance ledger and the site KPI table). It refreshes from your dbt marts on a schedule. The AI can read this dataset and nothing else.
  2. One definition per KPI. Each board-pack KPI (revenue, contribution, occupancy, churn and so on) is written down once, in dbt, with the formula Finance and Operations agree. The AI asks for "Soho contribution, September", and the definition does the maths. It never invents its own formula.
  3. Business context. Your board pack, KPI glossary, targets and "why this KPI matters" notes are loaded as reference material, so answers carry meaning: "Soho is 62% under its revenue budget, and occupancy is the main reason."
  4. The assistant. A chat interface, behind Work.Life sign-in, where a leader types a question. The assistant chooses the right KPIs, runs the queries on ai_safe, and replies with the number, a short explanation, the definition used and the data's freshness. If it can't answer reliably, it says so.

A scheduled weekly digest and a small set of alerts provide the proactive insight. The LLM runs inside your Google Cloud project, so data doesn't leave your environment for a third-party app.

Q5 · Security

Scoring: is it safe, and do you understand what must never be connected?

Access is through Work.Life accounts only, the AI can read only the controlled copy, and everything runs inside your own Google Cloud project.

  • Access control: single sign-on with your existing Work.Life identity provider, limited to a named leadership group. No shared or local logins. Access reviewed monthly by Operations & IT Systems.
  • Data isolation: the assistant uses its own service account with read-only access to ai_safe. It has no access to raw tables, staging models, source systems or any other dataset. It can only run read queries.
  • Safe data only: a written allow-list of tables and columns, following the same rule as your current reporting extracts and signed off by you. Names, emails, phone numbers and card numbers stay out. Door-access data, if used at all, is aggregated (for example "visits per company per week"), never individual tap events.
  • Never connect: the HR system or any people data; raw personal data; live source systems; payment or bank details; email inboxes and shared drives. Staff costs appear only as finance totals.
  • The model: served through your Google Cloud project on enterprise terms where your data isn't used for training. Every question, query and answer is logged, so you can audit what was asked and what data was used.

For your internal AI usage policy, we'd suggest: use only approved tools for company data; never paste member or staff personal data into public AI tools; treat AI answers as a draft until the sources are shown; name an owner for each approved tool; review the policy every six months.

Q6 · Accuracy

Scoring: this is the question they care about most. They wrote "if it confidently returns a wrong figure, trust is lost".

We treat accuracy exactly as you do: as a launch gate. Nobody relies on the assistant until it has passed a test against your actual results, agreed in advance with Finance and Operations.

  1. Prevent: the AI never does its own arithmetic. Every figure comes from a query against an agreed KPI definition. Answers show the definition, the period and the data's freshness ("September is not yet closed in NetSuite").
  2. Refuse: if a question can't be answered reliably (missing definition, unclosed period, data not in the copy) the assistant says so instead of guessing. An honest "I can't answer that reliably" is a pass. A confident wrong number is a fail.
  3. Test: we build a set of 50 golden questions with you: 30 board-pack KPI questions (tier 1) and 20 "why" questions (tier 2). Finance and Operations write down the correct answers from closed months in NetSuite and the board pack.
  4. Gate: our proposed bar, to agree with your leadership team: tier 1 at least 29 of 30 correct and zero wrong; tier 2 at least 16 of 20 judged correct by your reviewers, with no wrong figures; and every question asked three times gives the same figure.
  5. Keep checking: after go-live, the golden set re-runs automatically after every change and every month after Finance closes the period. A failure stops the release.

In practice, the "accuracy against actuals" check is simple: each month, when Finance closes the books, the assistant answers the tier 1 questions for that month and the results are compared with the board pack figures. Any difference is investigated before the board pack goes out.

Q7 · Resilience

Scoring: will it break when HubSpot takes over member data?

The assistant never sees a source system. It sees business tables (sites, members, contracts) and KPI definitions. If a source changes, only the dbt models that read that source change; the assistant, its definitions and its tests stay the same.

Your own example: as HubSpot becomes the source of truth for members, the dbt staging models switch from Nexudus to HubSpot for member fields, and a crosswalk table (member_xref) keeps one stable ID per member across both. The dim_member table the assistant reads keeps the same columns and meaning. The golden questions prove nothing changed: if they pass before and after the switch, the swap is safe. No prompt, KPI definition or test mentions "Nexudus" or "HubSpot" by name.

Interview Questions

Q: A client sends a scoping brief with 10 numbered questions. How do you structure your response?

"Exactly their 10 headings, in their order, each starting with a one-line answer. A half-page summary at the top. Numbers in tables at the levels they asked for. An appendix of assumptions and open questions. They'll compare suppliers side by side, so I make that comparison easy."

Q: You don't know the client's user count or accuracy bar, but they want a cost and an approach. What do you do?

"State a clear assumption, price on it, and say what changes if it's wrong. For example: 'we've assumed 10 leadership users; each extra 10 users adds about £2,000 a year in usage'. Then list the decision as something we need from them, with a date. That's what the brief asked for, and it's more useful than refusing to give a number."

Q: Why put "hard parts" in the summary of a sales document?

"Because this client explicitly said honest feedback is worth more than an optimistic pitch. Being upfront about the risks, like agreeing definitions on time, makes every other claim more credible. And they'd find those risks in month two anyway."

Q: The client's main fear is a confidently wrong number. What's the single most important design decision that addresses it?

"The LLM never calculates. Every figure comes from a query against an agreed KPI definition, and the answer shows that definition and the data's freshness. Then a refusal mode for anything it can't answer reliably, and a launch gate tested against their own closed months."

Q: Why not include an optional price for Phase 2 to show ambition?

"The brief says not to. Adding it would break the like-for-like comparison and suggest we didn't read the brief. The ambition goes into the Scalability answer instead: showing that each Phase 2 item reuses the Phase 1 foundations."
